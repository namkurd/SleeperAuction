Exactly how a grade is decided

Every graded pick is priced into a tier (A–E: where its price ranked among every pick at that position in that draft) and its result lands in a band (where it finished among every NFL player at that position that season). The grade is just that tier crossed with that band — the pricier the tier, the higher the bar for a good grade.

Every band — including Top 5 and Top 10 — scales to each position's real scarcity. Being the 9th-best player at a position only means something once you know how many were actually startable: 9th of 10 is barely droppable, 9th of 40 is a clear weekly starter. So "good enough to start," "elite," and everything between scale off how many roster spots the position actually fills league-wide — a league with one starting QB slot has only as many startable QBs as it has teams, while two starting RB/WR slots plus a shared flex make far more RB/WR finishes startable (30 in a 10-team league, 36 in a 12-team one). A QB or TE is judged against its own, usually much smaller, startable pool — not a flex position's bigger one. A WR/TE flex slot only widens the WR pool, since a tight end almost never wins it, and superflex only widens the QB pool:

Two examples: Jay Cutler, QB14 for $1 in a 10-team, one-QB season — 14th is past that year's 10 startable quarterbacks, so no price makes it better than 😐 Meh. And Rob Gronkowski, TE9 at a roughly median TE price in that same 10-team, one-TE era — 9th is barely inside the 10 startable tight ends, not a difference-maker, so a middling price for it tops out at 😐 Meh rather than the 🔥 Banger a flat "top 10" cutoff used to hand it. And Zach Ertz, TE19 for $1 in 2022: the W/T flex slot that year didn't make TE19 startable, so even at the minimum price it's a 😐 Meh, not a Hit. Pat Freiermuth, TE7 for $1 that same year, is a ✅ Hit — a back-end starter at the minimum price. A few guardrails on top of the grid: a premium price (tier A or B) that finishes at least as high as it was priced — the 4th tight end taken finishing 4th, say — is never worse than a ✅ Hit, since he delivered exactly what he cost. At a middling price (tier C/D), reaching Top 5/Top 10 only earns a Banger when it was a real steal: either the price was close to that position's cheapest pick that year (within $5, since the floor is almost always $1), or he finished at least a quarter of the position's drafted pool better than his price rank (Josh Jacobs, $9 and the 23rd RB taken in 2022, finishing RB3). Otherwise the finish just matched the price, which is a Hit, not a Banger. And a pick that cost $5 or less can never be branded a full Fail, only a Bust. Finishing #1 at the position is always 🔥 Banger regardless of price. Kickers, defenses, and picks without a season-finish rank yet aren't graded.

🩼 Injury pulls a real bad-luck case out of Fail: if the player took the field for 7 games or fewer that season — less than half of it — a Fail becomes an Injury instead, and it's dropped from Overall, Hit Rate, and Miss Rate entirely (it still gets its own column, and its share is of this manager's Fail-or-worse picks, not all picks). A $5-or-less pick that's already capped at Bust by the guardrail above never reaches Injury either — too little was risked either way.
